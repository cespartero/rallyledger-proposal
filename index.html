Here’s the proposal in one line: we run a Club Championship Series across the season, and RallyLedger runs it for us. It’s for the club, built by the club: it was made by one of our own members, around the way we actually play, not bought off the shelf. Think of it as the club noticeboard, the scorer’s clipboard and the trophy ladder, all in one place on your phone. It replaces the clipboard, the whiteboard and the spreadsheet. Before a session it’s the fixture list and RSVP sheet. On the night it’s the scorer. Across the season it keeps the ladder. The rest of this talk walks through each part.

So why do this at all? Four reasons. First, camaraderie: a season gives everyone a shared story. With rotating partners you play alongside lots of different members, and everyone is following the same ladder, the same way a footy club rallies around its season. Second, income: championship points reward turning up, and dropped results mean a missed week is never a reason to give up. Players have a reason to keep coming back, and regular attendance means steady session fees for the club. It works like a loyalty card. Third, a baseline: every player builds a results history and a Club Rating. That tells us where everyone stands, helps us place newcomers and set up fair divisions, a bit like a golf club’s handicap register. And fourth, growth: if the series takes off, people will hear about it, and anyone who wants a shot at the championship has a reason to join our club. Like a busy café, a bit of buzz brings more people through the door.

If you’ve played in a ladder league, a lot of this will feel familiar: a season of regular play, a ladder you can check, games against people around your level, and the chance to move up. The twist is in how you climb. There are no challenge matches to organise: you just turn up on the night and the app does the draw, with rotating partners so it stays social. And instead of swapping places when you win or lose, you collect championship points that only ever go up, with your weakest nights dropped. So you get the fun of chasing the ladder without the worry of sliding down it.

Tournaments and sessions

How a night is ranked

You’re ranked on your own record. Ties settle in this order:

1

Most wins

Games won on the night.

2

Fewest losses

Matters when people played a different number of games.

3

Point difference

Points you scored minus points scored against you.

4

Points scored

The last tie-breaker: total points you won.

Like a sports table

Wins first, then goal difference, then goals scored.

Even though you partner different people every round, the app keeps a personal record for you: your wins, your losses and your points. At the end of the night everyone is lined up from first to last. If two people are level on wins, it looks at who lost fewer, then at point difference, then at total points scored. It’s exactly like a league table settling teams on the same points with goal difference.

Dropped results

Only your best nights count

With “Best 5 of 8”, the ladder adds up your five best sessions. Your worst nights, and any you missed, are simply left out.

Away? No penalty.

Miss up to 3 of 8 sessions for holidays, work or injury and lose nothing.

Off night? Dropped.

A bad night only counts if it’s one of your best.

Extra nights only help

Every extra night you play is another chance to replace a low score.

Like school tests

The teacher drops your lowest marks, so one bad day doesn’t define your term.

This is the drop-results rule, and it’s the part people like most. The tournament decides how many sessions count, for example the best 5 out of 8. Your best five nights are added up and the rest are ignored. So if you’re away for a couple of weeks you’re not punished, and if you have a shocker one night it just falls off the bottom. And because more nights give you more chances to post a good score, regular attendance still pays. By default the app counts roughly two-thirds of the planned sessions.

Finals

Finals night: a knockout to finish the season

Seeded by the ladder

Your place in the standings decides your seed, so the season’s top players meet the lowest seeds first.

Knockout format

Single or double elimination, by division or across the whole club, with an optional singles showdown.

Ladder stays put

Finals don’t add championship points or move anyone between divisions.

Like AFL finals

Finish high on the ladder to earn a better draw, then it’s win and you’re through.

Many seasons end with a finals night. The app builds a knockout bracket for you. Seeds come straight from the ladder, so a strong season earns an easier first match, just like the top four in the AFL. The tournament manager picks the format: one loss and you’re out, or double elimination where you get a second life, and whether it’s per division or the whole club. Finals sit on top of the season: they don’t change the points ladder.

Divisions

Divisions keep games close

A tournament can split players into divisions, such as Division 1 and Division 2, so you mostly play people near your level.

Set by organisers

Managers place each player in a division. Players can’t pick their own.

Ranked within it

On the night each division plays and is ranked separately, with its own winner.

Courts your way

Divisions can share all the courts, or each can have its own courts.

Like grades in local footy

A grades and B grades play their own games on the same day, at the same ground.

Divisions are about keeping games competitive and fun. Instead of a beginner being drawn against our strongest player, the club can split into groups. Organisers decide who goes where; players don’t choose their own division. On the night, each division plays among itself and has its own results. Courts can be shared out round by round, or each division can have its own block of courts. Think of A grade and B grade playing on the same day at the same ground.

Divisions

Earn your way up, automatically

When switched on, the app moves players once they’ve earned it.

Moving up

Top of your division, again and again

Finish in the top spots of your division for a run of sessions in a row, for example top 1 twice running, and you move up.

Moving down

Bottom of your division, again and again

Finish in the bottom spots for the same run of sessions and you move down to find closer games.

Like soccer leagues

Promotion and relegation, but decided over a few weeks rather than one bad night.

When there’s more than one division, the club can turn on automatic promotion and relegation. The rule is about consistency: you have to finish at the top of your division for a set number of sessions in a row to go up, or at the bottom for that many in a row to go down. Organisers choose the numbers, for example top one twice running. A single fluke night never moves anyone. It’s promotion and relegation like the English soccer leagues, just judged over a short streak instead of one game. Finals nights never count toward this.

The result

Players only ever see their season heading upward, so it’s rewarding to keep turning up.

This is the big question: why points and not a pure rating ladder? Two reasons. First, rating ladders already exist: other leagues and apps like DUPR do exactly that, so offering the same thing gives members nothing new. Second, and more important, a rating works like a share price. It goes up and down with every game, so a hard draw or an off night can undo weeks of progress. That’s discouraging, especially for newer or social players. Championship points work like a savings jar: every night adds to it, nothing takes away from it, and with dropped results your worst nights don’t even count. Everyone’s season only goes up, which keeps people coming back.

To be clear, the app does keep a skill rating, called the Club Rating, and it does move up and down with results. But it isn’t what you’re competing for. Think of it like a golf handicap: it helps organisers set up fair games and put people in the right division, while the trophy is decided by the points ladder. New players are given a starting level when they join, beginner through to expert, and the rating settles in quickly over their first four sessions.

There are four levels of access, and a sports club is a good way to think about them. Visitors are the spectators in the stands: anyone can look at the standings and watch a live session without logging in. Players have a login: they get their own stats page, can RSVP, and can change their own display name and password, but nobody else’s. Session managers are the umpires and scorers: they run the night, from check-in to publishing results, and they can add sessions, look after the player list and put people in divisions. Tournament managers are the committee: they set up the season and its rules, and they’re the only ones who hand out logins and manager accounts.

No login? You can still play, score points and appear on the ladder. A login just unlocks your own stats and RSVPs.

Like a clubhouse key

The committee cuts the key, you can change the code, and a lost key is simply replaced.

Logins are handed out by tournament managers, a bit like the committee cutting clubhouse keys. First, the player is added to the club roster with a starting level. Then a tournament manager creates their login: a username or email, plus a temporary password of at least eight characters. You pass that on to the player, and they can change the password themselves whenever they like. If someone forgets their password, a manager just sets a new one; if someone leaves, the login can be switched off without losing their results. And remember: you don’t need a login to play. It just unlocks your personal page and RSVPs.
